DealerPulse · Design System 01 of 03

Ledger: a design system and the Executive Overview.

An analytics instrument for the group CEO of a five-branch Toyota dealership in India. Every figure on these screens is computed from dealership_data.json (510 leads, Jun–Dec 2025) by the analytics layer built in the previous session — nothing here is placeholder.

1440 primary 1024 secondary IBM Plex Sans + Mono, embedded Light + dark
01

The read

The primary user opens this on a laptop for ninety seconds between meetings, and on an iPad in a car. He does not explore. He wants to know what is wrong, what it costs, and who owns it. That single fact rules out an exploratory dashboard: the screen must state a conclusion and then show its working, in that order.

So the hero is not a KPI row. It is a written verdict — one sentence, large, with the rupee figure inline and one action. Everything below it exists to let a sceptical reader check that sentence. The five branch managers who use this defensively get the same screen; their branch is one row in the comparison, and they will find it in about a second.

Reference class is a financial instrument, not a startup dashboard: hairlines instead of cards, tabular figures everywhere, near-square corners, and colour used only where it carries meaning. The ground is light rather than terminal-dark, because the secondary context is a tablet in Indian daylight.

Why red is rationed

Group attainment is 12.4% against target. If red meant "below target", every branch would be red on every screen and the colour would stop meaning anything within one session. Red is therefore reserved for rupees the group has already earned but not collected — the ₹8.59 Cr of stalled orders — where it stays rare and keeps its force.

03

Type

IBM Plex Sans · IBM Plex Mono

IBM Plex Sans for language, IBM Plex Mono for every numeral. Plex was drawn as an institutional engineering face rather than a startup geometric, it carries true tabular lining figures, and it has a Devanagari sibling — which matters for a product that will eventually carry Indian-language rep and customer names. Both faces are embedded in this document as woff2, so what you see is the specified face, not a fallback.

Numerals are set in Mono rather than the sans's tabular figures. In a table of rupee values across five branches, monospace guarantees alignment by construction and gives the screen the ledger texture the reference class calls for.

Near-square throughout. Pill shapes and rounded-lg read as consumer software; a ledger does not.

Elevation is not decoration

Panels get a 1px rule and no shadow. Shadow is permitted on exactly two surfaces, because they genuinely float above the page: the lead drawer and popovers (filter menus, the severity explainer). Two tokens, no more.

05

Chart palette

validated, not eyeballed

I ran candidate palettes through a colour-blindness validator rather than trusting my eye. Two failed: a first set was too desaturated to read as distinct, and a second put green beside amber — ΔE 5.6 under protanopia, the classic red-green trap. That failure produced the better answer: this product barely needs categorical colour at all.

Almost every chart here is one of three jobs — focus versus context (Lakeside against the other four), magnitude (one hue, light to dark), or status. None of those is a categorical palette, so none is exposed to the categorical failure mode. The one true two-slot categorical pair passes all six checks in light and dark.

The focus / context rule

The series under investigation takes --c-focus and a heavier mark. Every other series takes --c-context, a neutral. The group benchmark is a --c-bench tick, never a filled series. Identity is carried by lightness and hue, so it survives any form of colour blindness — and by position and direct labels, so it survives greyscale printing.

06

Signature element — the delta rail

One idiom repeats at every level of the product: a hairline track carrying the group benchmark as a tick, the entity's value as a filled mark, and the gap printed as a signed tabular figure — with its rupee cost wherever money can be attached.

It appears in the branch comparison, on every funnel step, in the rep table, and on each Action Center row. The effect is that a manager learns to read one shape once and then reads the whole product. The verdict band at the top of the Overview is the same idea expressed in prose rather than geometry.

Target attainment

12.4% baseline under review

160 delivered against 1,426 targeted units

510 — every lead that exists

The seven-month target totals 1,426 units. Only 510 leads were created in the same period. Even at a 100% win rate the group could deliver 510 — the target is 2.8× total lead volume and cannot be reached by conversion at any performance level. It is shown for continuity and is excluded from every health signal on this screen.

1

Verdict band, not an alert banner. White surface, no coloured fill, no icon, no border tint. Authority comes from type size and the restraint around it. The rupee figure sits inline in Mono so the eye lands on it inside the sentence.

2

Evidence sits beside the claim. The five first-contact rails let a sceptical CEO verify the sentence without navigating. The claim and its proof share one horizontal band.

3

Vital signs are a strip, not cards. Five cells divided by hairlines, each carrying label, value, a comparison and a micro-shape. No floating tiles, no equal-weight grid of six.

4

Money at risk is the only red on the screen. It also carries a triangle glyph, so severity survives greyscale and colour blindness.

5

Two dot plots, one row order. Reading across a row shows cause and effect: the branch with the isolated first-contact mark has the isolated win-rate mark. Lakeside's gap is created by distance, not by an alert.

6

Targets are shown, then disarmed. The black tick marks the arithmetic ceiling. It converts a credibility problem into a visible, checkable fact.

What changes at 1024

1

Evidence moves below the sentence and compresses from five rails to five figures. The verdict keeps full weight — it is the reason the screen exists.

2

Vital signs drop from five to four. "Never contacted" is removed because the verdict sentence already states it; duplicating it costs a column that the remaining four need.

3

Filter bar collapses Model and Source into a single "More · 2" control. Period and Branch stay visible because they are the two a manager actually changes.

What is deprioritised

4

Dot plot loses its delta column and axis captions; the rails and values carry the comparison. Row order still encodes the ranking.

5

Money at risk shows three rows, not five, with branch demoted to a second line inside the name cell.

6

Where deals die and Lead source move below the fold as stacked full-width panels. They are supporting evidence, not the headline, and survive a scroll.

10

Defending the five

Five metrics, chosen so that together they answer did we make money, is the machine working, what is coming, what is stuck, and why — in that order.

MetricValueWhy a CEO needs it
Delivered revenue₹38.88 CrThe only number that is actually money in the door. Lagging, but it is the scoreboard.
Win rate31.4%Efficiency of the machine, independent of volume. Its micro-shape previews the whole story — four marks clustered, one isolated.
Open pipeline₹15.15 CrWhat is still winnable. Forward-looking, and the denominator for every follow-up action.
Money at risk₹8.59 CrAlready won, not collected. The single most actionable figure on the screen and the only one that earns red.
Never contacted119The leading indicator. It explains the four numbers above it and is the one a manager can change this week.

Cut, and why

Target attainment — the baseline is arithmetically unreachable, so promoting it would train the CEO to ignore a vital sign. Total leads — volume without outcome is vanity; it lives in the filter bar as a denominator instead. Delivered units — duplicates delivered revenue at lower resolution. Average deal value — interesting to a marketing head, not actionable for a CEO in ninety seconds; it lives in the Lead source panel where it earns its place beside win rate.

11

Branch comparison — the form I chose

Chosen — paired dot plot on a benchmark rail

Two aligned columns, one row per branch, ranked. Lakeside separates itself by distance along an axis — the most primitive visual comparison there is, and one that survives colour blindness, greyscale and a glance from four feet away. Reading across a row shows the cause beside the effect. The benchmark tick makes "normal" explicit rather than implied.

Rejected

Ranked bar with benchmark line — bar length invites summing branches, which is meaningless for a rate.
Slope chart — implies change over time; this is one period, and the shape would be read as a trend.
Small multiples of five funnels — accurate but needs ninety seconds on its own. It belongs on the branch screen, where it is the centrepiece.
Red alert icon on Lakeside — explicitly banned, and it would let the icon do work the data should do. A manager should see the gap before reading a label.
12

Rationale

The signature element is the verdict band: a generated sentence, set at 30px, that states the finding, names the money, and offers exactly one action — sitting on a plain white surface with no colour, no icon and no border. Everything else on the screen is evidence for it.

It fits a dealership CEO specifically because of how he uses the output. He is not going to interrogate a funnel between meetings; he is going to repeat one sentence to a branch manager on a phone call. Giving him that sentence — "forty-two per cent of Lakeside's leads are never called, and it is worth ₹5.4 crore" — is the entire product. A dashboard that made him assemble that sentence himself from six tiles would be a worse product that looked like more work. The quietness is the point: an alert banner is something you dismiss, and a typeset conclusion on white is something you believe.
