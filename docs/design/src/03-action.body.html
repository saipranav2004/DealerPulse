DealerPulse · Design System 03 of 03

The Action Center, and every state around it.

A prioritised work queue that opens every morning. If a manager clears this list, the business measurably improves. Plus the states, breakpoints, accessibility rules and shipping copy that separate a product from a demo.

01

The severity rule is visible

Ranking is value × lateness × stage risk. A hidden score would be worse than no score: a manager who cannot see why a row is at the top has no reason to trust the order, and will re-sort by whatever column they already understand. So the rule is a button on the header, and every row can show its own substitution.

Why is this at the top?

Stage weights: order placed 3.0 · negotiation 2.0 · test drive 1.5 · contacted 1.2 · new 1.0. Money already committed outranks money still being discussed.

Sorting by age alone would be wrong. These two rows show why:

04

Cold leads — thresholds from dwell medians

The brief asked for age thresholds derived from real stage dwell medians rather than round numbers. Doing that honestly produced a result worth stating plainly: a flat seven-day rule surfaces 35 open leads, but 32 of them are stalled orders already sitting in the first queue, and two of the remaining three are still inside the normal window for their stage. One lead is genuinely cold.

Threshold derivation

Waiting atMedian next moveCold after
New1 d2 d
Contacted5 d10 d
Test drive3 d6 d
Negotiation8 d16 d

Cold = twice the median time that stage normally takes to advance. A new lead untouched for three days is late; a lead in negotiation for twelve is not.

What this assumes

Lakeside's conversion after first contact stays where it is today — 13.0% of contacted leads reach delivery, against 44.6% across the group. Only the first step moves.

If those leads also converted at group rates, the same slider position gives +22 deliveries and ₹5.51 Cr — the figure behind the headline on the Overview. That result needs the whole funnel fixed, not just the phone calls, which is why it is not the number shown above.

1

The conservative number is the headline. A simulator that shows its most flattering result first is a sales tool, not an instrument. Leading with +2 deliveries and putting +22 in the assumption block — clearly explained — is what makes the +22 believable when a manager gets there.

Keyboard, touch, motion

Focus — 2px accent outline at 2px offset, on every interactive element. Never removed, never opacity-only.

Tab order — filter bar → tabs → bulk bar → each row's checkbox then its action → pagination. Row content is not a tab stop; the whole row is reachable via its primary action.

Contrast — body ink on surface 15.8:1, secondary 6.4:1, accent on white 6.9:1, critical on white 7.4:1. Chart marks clear 3:1 against their surface.

Touch — 44px minimum at 768. Checkboxes grow to 22px with a 44px hit area; icon buttons become full-width labelled buttons.

Reduced motion — the skeleton shimmer is decorative and is cut to a static fill. Row fade on action is cut. Nothing that conveys state relies on movement.

Rules this copy follows

Sentence case everywhere. Active voice with a plain verb — Chase delivery, not Delivery follow-up. An action keeps its name from button to confirmation: Assign becomes Assigned. No filler openers, no apologies in errors, no exclamation marks. Numbers are always lakh or crore. Nothing says "Oops" or "Something went wrong" — an error names the module, states the effect, and offers the retry.

10

Self-critique

What I would cut

The delivery-delays queue.

It is the weakest of the three and I would ship without it. All 47 rows are already delivered cars, so nothing in that queue can be actioned — a manager cannot un-delay a car that arrived in October. It is analysis wearing a queue's clothing, and putting it beside two lists where every row has a live next step teaches people that some tabs are worth ignoring, which is exactly how a work queue dies.

The content is real and worth keeping — seven named causes, with factory allocation and logistics accounting for 22 of 72 recorded delays. It belongs as a module on a supply-side screen, not as a third of the morning queue.

Runner-up: the cohort maturity flag. It is analytically essential and almost impossible to explain in a tooltip. I would keep it computing and show it only where December's shape would otherwise mislead.

Why this reads as bought, not submitted

It takes positions that cost it something. Attainment is 12.4% and the design says the target is broken and proves it arithmetically — 264 units demanded of a branch given 79 leads. The cold-leads queue shows one row where a flat seven-day rule would have shown thirty-five, because thirty-two were duplicates. A demo inflates; an instrument subtracts.

It says what it does not know. Fourteen records are admitted on every screen with their exact treatment. The what-if leads with +2 deliveries and explains why +22 needs more than phone calls. Actions say they save to the browser only.

It is built for one person's ninety seconds. The product resolves to a sentence a CEO can repeat on a phone call, and every screen below it exists to let him check that sentence in two clicks. Take-homes tend to produce six equal tiles and let the reader assemble the finding. Doing that assembly — and standing behind it in writing — is the whole job.
